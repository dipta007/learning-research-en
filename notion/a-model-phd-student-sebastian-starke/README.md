# A Model PhD Student: Sebastian Starke

> [Original Article](https://pengsida.notion.site/1713fe292ff1808eb33be93ea2d79ad9)

Over the last couple of days I read Sebastian Starke's series of papers on character control from his PhD, and learned a great deal ([my paper notes](https://pengsida.notion.site/1703fe292ff1809e92d2ff48f47e06de?pvs=25) ([a study note, not translated](../not-translated.md))). I do not know Sebastian Starke, but simply from reading his series of papers I came away deeply impressed, with many thoughts. I am recording my own thinking here so I can review and reflect on it later.

[Sebastian Starke](https://github.com/sebastianstarke) did his PhD in Taku Komura's group. He published one paper at SIGGRAPH every year of his PhD, and over five years built a character control system close to being commercialized, which is astonishing, and which hugely raised the usable level of motion systems in the games industry. On GitHub, his [AI4Animation](https://github.com/sebastianstarke/AI4Animation) has collected 7.4k stars. Academically, each of his SIGGRAPH papers takes on a harder industry problem than the last, researching deeper in one direction, and the final paper of his PhD, DeepPhase, finally took the SIGGRAPH best paper award, deservedly.

Reading his series of papers, you can see his research style:

1. **A very clear, long-term goal, and problem-driven research.** His papers are consistently solving all sorts of problems inside a character control system, from simple periodic motion control early on to complex motion control later, conquering one mountain after another in the character control direction.
2. **He can always find a valuable problem.** Perhaps benefiting from the long accumulation in Taku's group, Sebastian's papers can always find the highest-priority problem in character control, rather than spending effort on some problems that are inconsequential or not important yet.
3. **He fills gaps without fearing difficulty, and takes on problems with "no answer".** What is impressive about Sebastian is his willingness to attack hard problems and keep filling gaps. One major problem in character control is the complexity of the target motion. His senior labmate Daniel Holden's PFNN solved simple periodic motion; his Local Motion Phase pushed further and solved compositional motion; then DeepPhase, the SIGGRAPH best paper, solved complex aperiodic motion, very nearly settling the question of motion complexity.

   Compared with "target motion complexity", character control has other problems that are easier to publish on, such as multi-modal control. Sebastian did not go after those because they were easier to publish; he consistently kept his effort on the most important problems, the kind that have no well-established solution. His willingness to keep filling gaps without fearing difficulty is the key to his being able to move the industry forward.
4. **Extremely strong technical ability.** The first three traits let Sebastian concentrate his effort on the most important problems, but actually solving them rests on his own extremely strong technical ability: broad technical accumulation, deep technical thinking, and hardcore engineering. Sebastian must have worked through the whole technology stack around character control, including motion capture, fitting, animation, deep learning, Unity and so on.
5. **He tries new techniques early.** Sebastian's papers always try the frontier techniques of their moment; DeepPhase, for example, explored applying representation learning to motion. I believe that had he only followed the technical path of Local Motion Phase to solve control of complex aperiodic motion, such a large improvement would have been unlikely.

Having studied and summarized Sebastian Starke's research style carefully, I reflected on the mistakes I made during my own PhD. These mistakes also show up occasionally in some of the students I supervise, causing their intelligence not to be applied in the right place. The concrete mistakes:

1. **Lacking a long-term goal, not focusing on solving problems in one direction.** During my PhD I changed topics three times in the first three years: object pose estimation, instance segmentation, dynamic 3D reconstruction. Fortunately I settled in the end on dynamic 3D reconstruction. Had I focused on object pose estimation from the start, my research would have gone more smoothly, I could have done better work in the object pose field, and I could have had a label of my own fairly early.
2. **Tending to solve the easy problems, not looking for the most valuable ones.** In the third year of my PhD I thought dynamic human surface reconstruction was an easy problem to solve and to publish on, and spent half a year on the Animatable SDF project. Looking back now, that half year was a complete waste, and I learned nothing new. Had I been able to spend the time solving a valuable problem, such as improving the rendering quality of 3D humans, my technical level could have improved more and I would have had a better chance of larger impact in dynamic 3D reconstruction.
3. **Lacking the awareness to solve a problem to 99 percent.** After finishing Neural Body, I had raised rendering quality to a watchable level. At the time I was chasing what looked fancy, and went straight on to the animatable human problem. For those two years people from industry often raised with me that dynamic human rendering quality urgently needed improving, and unfortunately I did not go and solve it. Looking back now, in the dynamic human reconstruction field I only published a few papers without genuinely solving anything, and became someone who "only knows how to publish papers, with no real contribution to industry".
4. **Wasting time on problems that "have an answer".** Deep Snake, which I did in my second year, was aimed at a problem that the earlier paper Curve-GCN had already solved fairly well, and I was only patching things on top. It feels very pointless now, and in fact I was full of self-doubt while doing it. A project like that can only drain a person's enthusiasm for research: all cost, no benefit.
5. **Reading narrowly, accumulating little technique.** I did not have this problem myself, but I have seen some students without the habit of reading papers widely, which is a great shame.
6. **Not trying new techniques early.** During my PhD some new techniques rose, such as Transformer, diffusion models and video models. My thinking then was very strange: I always felt emerging techniques were hype, which was simply a Qing-dynasty mindset, and I spent much time on small patches to the existing technical framework. Had I been able to treat emerging techniques as a source of hope and looked at whether they could solve some hard problems in my own field, I should have been able to produce some better work. In fact my PVNet and Neural Body are exactly examples of using emerging techniques well, but at the time I completely had no idea why they were strong. Being able to produce those two pieces of work was entirely down to Prof. Zhou's good supervision: he gave me good problems and was firm that I should not use the old technical approach.

Writing this article, I summarized where Sebastian succeeded, and also reflected on many mistakes I made myself. I hope I can learn more actively from the strong people ahead of me, put my effort into solving important problems, and always be able to be on the front line of trying new techniques.
