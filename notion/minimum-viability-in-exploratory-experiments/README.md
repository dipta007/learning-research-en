# Exploratory Experiments Should Follow Minimum Viability

> [Original Article](https://pengsida.notion.site/2863fe292ff180759413f51ed1d1fdc3)

#### What minimum viability is

Minimum viability in research means using the least resources, time and effort to build a simplified experiment, model or prototype that is just enough to test the core scientific hypothesis or technical principle.

#### The benefits of minimum viability

The core importance: lower the risk, raise the efficiency. This is the most fundamental value of minimum viability (MV). Research is by nature an exploration of the unknown, full of uncertainty and the risk of failure.

- **Test the hypothesis quickly, avoid going in the wrong direction.** You may have a very clever idea, but it may hide a fatal logical flaw, or a practical constraint you have not considered. Discovering that it fundamentally cannot work only after investing a lot of time and effort in building a complex experimental system is a huge waste. The minimum viability approach lets you run a simple experiment quickly and answer this question first: is this idea possible in principle? If even the simple experiment fails, you can drop it or change direction decisively, saving a lot of valuable resources.
- **Use limited resources efficiently.** Research resources, meaning funding, equipment, student time and compute, are always scarce. Minimum viability forces you to focus on the most core question, and keeps you out of the trap of optimizing details and building a perfect system early in the project. Put your good steel into the blade's edge. *Translator's note: a Chinese idiom. It means spending scarce resources where they actually decide the outcome.*
- **Get feedback quickly and iterate forward.** What minimum viability produces is a first version that is crude but can work. You can use it to gather preliminary data, show it to your advisor and peers, apply for preliminary funding, or find problems in the design. From that early feedback you can iterate and improve with a target in mind, keeping the research route always evolving in the most efficient direction. This is a build-measure-learn loop.

#### How to apply the minimum viability rule concretely when running exploratory experiments

The core idea: test whether the idea is feasible at the lowest cost.

**Step one: define the standard for "feasibility" (make the goal explicit).**

Before starting any experiment, you have to answer clearly: what is this minimal experiment testing? What is the standard for success?

That "feasibility" standard has to be binary, explicit and measurable (yes or no, reached or not reached). It is the compass for this piece of exploration.

**Step two: design the "minimal" experiment (simplify to the extreme).**

The task at this step is to strip away every non-essential factor, and build the simplest system that can answer the core question from step one.

How to do it concretely: ask "if I only had one day, what is the simplest experiment I could run to test my idea?"

**Step three: execute and learn (act fast, focus on feedback).**

1. **Execute quickly.** The goal is to finish the experiment and get the raw data as soon as possible. Do not aim for flawless experimental records, though they must stay reproducible, and accept a certain roughness.
2. **Analyze the results against the standard.** Compare the data you got strictly against the feasibility standard set in step one.
3. **Make an explicit decision.** The result leads to only three clear courses of action:
   - **Success, the hypothesis is supported.** The core idea is proven feasible. The next step is to design a more rigorous, larger-scale experiment to consolidate the finding. You can start adding complexity and optimizing details.
   - **Failure, the hypothesis is overturned.** The core idea does not hold. This is a huge success, because you avoided a big pit at minimal cost. The next step is to analyze the reason for failure deeply: is the idea fundamentally wrong, or is there a problem with how it was implemented? On that basis, pivot decisively to a new idea, or stop this direction.
   - **Uncertain, the data is ambiguous.** The result neither supports nor overturns the hypothesis. This usually means the experiment was not designed well enough, and that the "feasibility" standard of minimum viability was not set clearly. The next step is to revisit step one and improve the experimental design so that it can give a clearer answer. You may need a more refined minimal experiment.
